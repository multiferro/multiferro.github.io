Research

Ferroics are materials with a switchable order: a polarization, a magnetization or a strain that an external field can reverse. I predict their properties from first principles and follow them at finite temperature with atomistic simulations, aiming at materials for cooling, sensing, memory and spin control.

Antiferroelectrics at small scale

Lead zirconate, PbZrO3, is the archetypal antiferroelectric: neighbouring dipoles cancel, so the crystal carries no net polarization until a strong field forces one. With collaborators at Georgia Tech, Queen's University Belfast and Trinity College Dublin, I study what this means when the material is a thin film or a nanostructure only a few unit cells across. The simulations predicted a critical thickness below which antiferroelectricity gives way to ferroelectricity, found ferrielectric phases between the two, and showed how polar phases with large strain can be reached with applied fields. This work is supported by an NSF U.S.–Ireland R&D Partnership award.

Hybrid and halide perovskites

Organic–inorganic hybrid perovskites and germanium halides such as CsGeX3 combine a ferroelectric polarization with a semiconducting band structure. Because the polarization can be switched, so can the Rashba and Dresselhaus spin splittings tied to it, which makes these materials candidates for electrically controlled spintronics. I use density functional theory to map how structure, polarization, band gap and spin texture respond to pressure, strain, chemistry and film thickness. Along the way the calculations turned up unusual mechanical responses, including negative linear compressibility and negative longitudinal piezoelectricity.

Caloric effects

Ferroelectrics heat up and cool down when an electric field, a stress or a magnetic field is applied or removed. These electrocaloric, elastocaloric and multicaloric effects are a route to solid-state cooling without refrigerant gases. I developed atomistic simulations that follow the caloric response directly, including at high frequency, and used them to establish scaling laws, to show how phase competition in antiferroelectrics can be exploited, and to identify a hidden variable behind the electrocaloric response of ferroics. The same approach predicted a sizeable elastocaloric effect in carbon nanotubes and graphene.

Relaxors and disorder

In relaxor ferroelectrics such as Ba(Ti,Zr)O3, chemical disorder smears the sharp phase transition into a broad, frequency-dependent response. Molecular dynamics simulations of these alloys trace how polar order evolves with composition and temperature, found that the polarization dynamics persists into the gigahertz range, and with machine learning showed that the relaxor state keeps a memory of the phases of its parent compounds. Related work with experimental groups addresses the central mode in (Ba,Sr)TiO3 and structural disorder near the critical point of BaTiO3.

Multiferroics

Bismuth ferrite, BiFeO3, is the best-known room-temperature multiferroic, with coupled ferroelectric and antiferromagnetic orders. Using a first-principles-based effective Hamiltonian, I computed its finite-temperature phase diagram, the paths its polarization and magnetic order follow under electric and magnetic fields, and the strain-driven phase transitions observed in epitaxial films. Later work identified a thermally mediated mechanism that enhances magnetoelectric coupling and explored isentropic magnetoelectric coupling in planar heterostructures.

  • Bridging multiferroic phase transitions by epitaxial strain in BiFeO3, Physical Review Letters (2010)
  • Electric-field-induced paths in multiferroic BiFeO3 from atomistic simulations, Physical Review Letters (2009)

Methods and low-dimensional materials

Across these projects I combine density functional theory with effective Hamiltonians and molecular dynamics fitted to first-principles data, which reaches the length and time scales of films, domains and switching at finite temperature. Current efforts include effective Hamiltonians for non-perovskite ferroelectrics, benchmarks of density functionals for ferroelectric oxides, and a relaxation approach to quantum-mechanical modelling of phase transitions. Work on low-dimensional systems covers carbon nanotube junctions, magnetic graphene, transition-metal dichalcogenide layers with intercalated magnetic atoms, and topological-insulator surfaces.
